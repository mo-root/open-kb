## The five relations

Every edge is stated **from the anchor outward** and is one of these.

**competitor** — head-on. A buyer evaluating the anchor would put this on the same shortlist and
pick one. Test: do they sell the same capability to the same buyer?

**substitute** — does the same job a completely different way. Not the same kind of product, so it
never appears in a comparison article next to the anchor. Test: if the buyer chose this, would the
anchor become unnecessary? *A managed service, a ready-made dataset, or hiring a contractor can all
substitute for a tool.*

This is the most-missed category and usually the most valuable, precisely because the comparison
content that surfaces competitors cannot surface substitutes. If your map has no substitutes, you
have mapped the shortlist, not the market.

**shaper** — the incumbent everyone positions against, the standard the category is defined by, or
the infrastructure the whole market sits on. Not competing with the anchor, and not noise. Test: if
this company changed its behaviour, would the others have to react?

Without this relation a map keeps answering *who competes* when the question was *who matters*.

**dependency** — what the anchor is built on. Test: would the anchor stop working without it?

**integration** — what the anchor plugs into, or what plugs into it. Test: do they appear together
in a working setup without either being required?

## Choosing

`dependency` and `integration` are the pair most often confused: built **on** versus plugged **into**.
`competitor` and `substitute` are the other: same kind of thing versus same outcome.

Pick the closest honest one and say why in the reason. Guessing confidently is worse than choosing
approximately and explaining — a reader can correct a stated reason and cannot correct a bare label.

## The reason on an edge

Say **how** it relates, not that it resembles. "Similar company" is worth nothing to a reader.

> ✗ Adjacent player in the same space.
> ✓ Sells the fraud-scoring step the anchor bundles into checkout, as a standalone product — so a
>   buyer who wants only that step buys here instead.

The second sentence tells a reader what to do. The first tells them nothing.
