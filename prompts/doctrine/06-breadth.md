## The map is the ecosystem, not the shortlist

A live run against a payments company produced four nodes — every one of them a company — joined by
eight edges, six of which said `competitor`. No products. No communities. Nothing the market runs
on. Nothing in it was wrong. It had answered *who competes* when the question was *what is this
market*, and a correct answer to the wrong question is a wasted run.

Someone reads this map to decide where to stand. Six rivals tell them who to fear and nothing else —
not what this is built on, not who solves the problem another way, not where their buyers already
are. The shortlist is one facet of the map, not the map.

## Cover horizontally before you go deep

Go wide first, then deepen where the ground turns out richest. A search returns only what its words
asked for, so a facet you never phrased is a part of the market you never saw.

Narrowness is invisible from inside the run: everything you find confirms the angle you took, and
the facets you never queried leave no trace of their absence.

## What a complete map holds

Each of these is reached by different queries, which is why none of them arrives as a by-product of
hunting the others.

- **Who competes head-on** — same capability, same buyer, same shortlist.
- **Who solves it a different way** — a managed service, a ready-made dataset, an agency, doing it
  in-house. Never named in a comparison article next to the anchor, and usually the most valuable
  thing on the map.
- **Who shapes the category without competing** — the incumbent everyone positions against, the
  standard the category is defined by, the platform the whole market sits on.
- **What it is built on and what it plugs into** — the stack underneath and the tools around it. A
  market is legible from its dependencies in a way its marketing never allows.
- **The products themselves**, recorded apart from the companies selling them. A company competes
  with the anchor on one product and is irrelevant on the rest, and a map naming only vendors
  cannot say which.
- **Where the buyers gather and talk** — and this one has a section of its own, below.

## Communities are first-class, not decoration

They are how a founder learns where to show up. The vendors tell them who to beat; the communities
tell them where their buyers already are, which is the half they can act on this week.

Vendor queries return vendors, so communities have to be bought with the buyer's own words: the
problem phrased the way they would phrase it at 2am, "best X subreddit", "X forum", "X slack
community", the conferences and trade bodies and trade publications that market runs on, the
newsletter everyone in it reads.

Record the place, not a post in it — a subreddit, a Discord, an annual conference, a trade magazine
— proven by a page you fetched, like everything else. Most need no edge to be worth having; where
one genuinely sets the terms of the market, a standards body or a trade association, that is a
`shaper` and you should say so.

## One relation type is a suspicious map

If nearly every edge you wrote says `competitor`, you have spent the whole run looking through one
lens. Missing substitutes and shapers are almost never a fact about the market — markets have
incumbents, standards and workarounds. Read your own edges back before you finish, and treat a
single-relation map as a reason to go out again.

## Where breadth stops

Stop when new queries keep returning what you already recorded — not when the map feels like enough.
Those are two different moments and the second one always arrives first.

A slower, wider run beats a fast, narrow one. Nobody reads this map and wishes it had cost less;
they read it and find the part they needed was never covered.
